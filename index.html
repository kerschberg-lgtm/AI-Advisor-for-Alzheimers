The four ways AI helps

Where artificial intelligence fits in the Alzheimer's journey

AI is not one thing. It is a set of techniques that find patterns in large amounts of data. In Alzheimer's, those patterns show up in brain scans, blood tests, speech, movement, and daily routines.

Earlier detection

AI models read MRI and PET scans, retinal images, blood biomarkers, and even patterns in speech or typing to flag early signs of Alzheimer's, sometimes years before a traditional diagnosis.

Some tools are in clinics now; many are still in research

Finding new treatments

Machine learning screens millions of molecules, predicts how proteins like amyloid and tau fold, and helps identify which existing drugs might be repurposed. It also makes clinical trials faster by matching the right participants.

Mostly behind the scenes in labs and drug companies

Daily care and safety

Voice assistants that give gentle reminders, sensors that notice changes in sleep or wandering, and chat tools that help caregivers answer "what do I do when..." questions at 2 a.m.

Available today, with wide differences in quality

Research and prediction

Models that forecast how quickly the disease may progress for an individual, helping families plan and helping clinicians personalize care and choose who benefits most from new drugs.

Promising, but not yet standard practice

Common questions

Straight answers about AI and Alzheimer's

Can an AI tool tell me if I have Alzheimer's?

No. Some AI tools can estimate risk or flag patterns that deserve a closer look, but a diagnosis still requires a clinician who examines you, reviews your history, and orders tests such as cognitive assessments, blood biomarkers, brain imaging, or spinal fluid analysis. Treat any app or website that claims to diagnose Alzheimer's on its own with caution.

Are AI blood tests for Alzheimer's real?

Blood tests that measure proteins such as p-tau217 are real and increasingly used by specialists. AI is often used to combine those results with age, genetics, and cognitive scores to produce a clearer risk picture. Availability, insurance coverage, and interpretation vary, so these tests are best ordered and explained by a memory clinic or neurologist.

Is it safe to ask a chatbot for caregiving advice?

For general questions, such as how to respond to repeated questions or how to make a home safer, chatbots can be a helpful starting point. They can also be confidently wrong. Use them to prepare questions for a professional, not to make medical decisions, and never share another person's private health information with a tool whose privacy policy you have not read.

How is AI used in the new Alzheimer's drugs?

Anti-amyloid antibodies such as lecanemab and donanemab were developed with traditional biology, but AI helped along the way: analysing PET scans to measure amyloid, identifying trial participants faster, and monitoring safety signals like ARIA (brain swelling or small bleeds) on MRI. The next generation of candidates is being discovered with AI from the start.

What should I look for before trusting an AI health tool?
  • Has it been tested in peer-reviewed studies with people like you?
  • Is it cleared or approved by a regulator such as the FDA, or is it "wellness" only?
  • Does it explain its limits and tell you when to see a professional?
  • What does it do with your data, and can you delete it?
  • Does your clinician recognise it or recommend it?
Does AI replace doctors, nurses, or caregivers?

No. The most useful AI in Alzheimer's care works alongside people: it reads scans so that radiologists can focus on hard cases, drafts care plans that nurses refine, and handles reminders so that caregivers have more energy for connection. Human judgment, touch, and presence remain at the centre of good care.

About this site

Why AI Advisor for Alzheimer's exists

Headlines about artificial intelligence and Alzheimer's swing between miracle cures and dystopian warnings. Families, clinicians, and researchers deserve something calmer: a plain-language guide to what the technology actually does, how mature each use is, and how to tell a trustworthy tool from a hopeful one.

The content here is written for a general reader, reviewed against primary sources, and updated as the field changes. It is not medical advice. If you or someone you love is experiencing memory changes, please speak with a healthcare professional.
